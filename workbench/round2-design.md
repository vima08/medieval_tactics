# Round 2 tactical design critique

## Evidence and scope

I read the current engine, roster and battle UI, inspected the attack forecast capture in `round2-shots/03b-attack-preview.png`, and inspected the verified `round2-replays` summary and command streams. I then completed two Highland matches with a player directed blue side against the hard red AI. The blue side used a simple, disclosed objective plan: take open flags, use forecasted attacks when available, and guard or trap useful approaches. Every choice, its reason, and the board before it are in the journals. These are headless rules matches, not browser click sessions; the opponent was the game AI. Both resulting command streams reproduced the complete final state through `replayGame`.

## What produces decisions

- **Height: partly.** Rising consumes extra movement and can block routes; high ground adds one damage and archers firing uphill lose one. The UI exposes elevation, route cost, line of sight and forecast damage. In the tutorial attack capture the selected archer's shot is forecast at one damage with the uphill penalty spelled out. Highland's useful flags sit along the central crossing, so my objective plan mostly selected central approaches. Neither match showed a decisive high ground attack or fall kill. Height matters tactically when it enters a fight, but these two matches do not show it steering the winning plan.
- **Six classes: yes, unevenly.** All six appear across the two Highland rosters. Shield durability and guard made it a reliable flag holder; in the first match blue's shield occupied the bridge flag at (8,9), survived at 2 HP, and blue won. Spear push displaced red's engineer from (9,9) on turn 6, directly affecting occupation. Archer range produced forecasted damage and killed the opposing scout in the second match. The scout got to the crossing early but died on turn 2 in both player matches. The engineer placed one trap in the second match but fell to ranged fire before it became an objective holder. Sword held blue's home flag in the first match. These are distinct jobs, but objective holding rewards durable bodies more consistently than fast or utility ones.
- **Combos: present, narrow.** The shield plus sword held the home and bridge points while a spear push displaced an occupier. The engine also supports guard armor, archer height bonuses, traps, terrain collapse and artifact adjacency. The journal demonstrates the positional push and forecasted attacks, not a broad combo chain. In these matches the central question was whether two bodies remained on flags when red ended its turn.
- **Variants and artifacts: roster tradeoffs, limited realized impact.** The tower shield has 11 HP and one move; the escort shield has 9 HP and three move. That difference visibly slowed the customized challenger toward its second flag, while the escort reached (8,9) in the winning match. `standard` and `ember` filled the challenger budget, but no recorded attack shows their bonus deciding damage; the engineer's attack on turn 4 forecast one damage. The artifacts are valid roster decisions, but this sample does not show them compensating for lost tempo.
- **Previews: useful and generally actionable.** The battle UI shows reachable cells, threat tint, route cost, exact attack damage, push and hazard consequences. The tutorial image shows the uphill archer penalty before confirmation. The human choice journals used `previewAction` to select legal attacks, including the spear push; both full command streams replayed exactly. The screenshot also shows small forecast text and a thin attack line, so visual scanning remains slower than the accuracy of the information warrants.
- **Objective and pacing: strategically sharp, communicated incompletely.** Four flags require occupying two at the instant red ends its turn. This gave both sides an immediate spatial plan; the first player match had 83 commands over eight rounds and the second 68 over seven. Preset 0 still won both sides of the matchup, in line with all eight distinct head to head AI setups in [the verified round 2 audit](round2-balance.md). The two player results support that pattern without proving the roster alone causes it.

## Biggest flaw and correction to test

**The control score behaves as a consecutive streak, but the UI describes it as earned points.** `endTurn` increments a side that holds at least two flags and sets its entire score to zero otherwise. The objective panel says two of four points give one point at full round end and asks players to reach five. It does not say that losing the hold erases all progress. In the first player match, red stood at 4/5 after round 6 and at 0/5 after round 7, while blue advanced from 3/5 to 4/5. That one turn changed the perceived finish line. A player can make a good disruption move, but the magnitude of its effect is hard to plan from the stated rule.

**Testable correction:** keep points cumulative: at each full round, add one for a side holding two flags and add zero otherwise. Update the objective panel to state this explicitly. Replay the default and swapped rosters with equal difficulty and two player plans. Compare winner, rounds, time spent with each side holding two flags, and how often a 4/5 leader loses. If the intended design is a streak victory, retain the reset but label it *consecutive rounds* and show the threatened streak loss before red ends the round; then test whether players can predict the score change. The cumulative rule is my preferred first prototype because it makes the displayed score mean what the panel currently implies.

The matchup imbalance deserves a separate roster experiment: swap preset 1's engineer for a sword while keeping its other four slots fixed, then run both sides at equal AI difficulty and inspect flag occupation, as proposed in the balance audit. That is independent of the scoring clarity issue.
